BUSINESS SYSTEMS · TECHNOLOGY STRATEGY · ERP EVALUATION

Choosing the business system—not just the software.

I led a company-wide technology strategy and ERP evaluation for a formula-driven manufacturer, comparing the operating model each platform would create across data continuity, specialized workflows, companion systems, implementation risk, and long-term support.

ROLE

Project lead · systems strategist · analyst

SCOPE

7 functional areas · 4 platform finalists

OUTCOME

Preferred platform selected · execution deferred

ProblemArchitectureEvidenceRecommendationOutcome

THE REAL DECISION

The company was not choosing between four interchangeable ERPs.

The existing system had genuine capability gaps, but replacement was not the only path under discussion. Some needs could be addressed by custom tools. One strategic view favored building a new system from scratch. Other stakeholders questioned whether the core ERP needed to change at all.

Each option implied a different boundary between purchased software, custom development, integrations, manual process, and internal ownership. Comparing features alone would have hidden the consequences of those choices.

Project thesis

The right technology architecture had to support the company’s underlying business system—and remain realistic to implement, validate, govern, and maintain.

A weakness at any handoff could surface later as quoting error, purchasing risk, production rework, compliance exposure, or delayed delivery. The technology strategy therefore had to preserve meaning across the entire lifecycle.

THE ARCHITECTURE CROSSROADS

The strongest manufacturing tool could still create the most fragmented enterprise.

PATH A · INDUSTRY-SPECIFIC

Specialized depth first

  • Built-in formulation, manufacturing, and quality workflows
  • Faster fit for regulated production
  • Separate finance, HR, quoting, or CRM systems may remain
  • More integrations, vendors, and support boundaries

Less specialized configuration now → more system complexity later

PATH B · FULL ERP

Enterprise unification first

  • Broader finance, sales, purchasing, and operations coverage
  • Fewer companion applications and data boundaries
  • Specialized formula and compliance workflows require configuration
  • Greater design effort before regulated execution is ready

More configuration now → simpler system landscape later

DEMONSTRATED EVIDENCE

A persuasive demo was not the same as proven workflow coverage.

Functional teams scored workflows they actually saw. I kept user confidence separate from demo coverage: a system could feel intuitive in a narrow demonstration yet leave important workflows untested, while a broader demonstration could reveal adoption friction.

IMPLEMENTATION RISK

Vendor behavior during evaluation was evidence about delivery.

I independently tracked responsiveness, scheduling stability, communication ownership, coordination burden, and proposal ambiguity. These were not treated as soft impressions: they informed the likelihood that discovery, migration, validation, training, and implementation would remain controlled.

RECOMMENDATION

The preferred platform offered the strongest overall operating model—not the deepest capability in every department.

SELECTED ARCHITECTURE

Full ERP

Enterprise unification with configured support for specialized operations.

PREFERRED DIRECTION · 01

Why this path emerged

  1. Strongest balance of company-wide coverage and specialized workflow fit
  2. Lowest long-term architecture complexity among the viable finalists
  3. Most reliable observed implementation profile
  4. Clearer vendor ownership for validation and delivery
  5. Simpler foundation for unified data and future growth

Decision status: the preferred platform was selected, while purchase and implementation were deferred as the company prioritized near-term production expansion and equipment investment.

WHAT THE PROJECT PRODUCED

Decision readiness, even without immediate implementation.

BEFORE

Competing technology opinions

  • Replace, retain, extend, or build from scratch
  • Department needs evaluated in isolation
  • Feature appeal obscuring architecture consequences
  • Implementation risk largely implicit
→

AFTER

Structured technology direction

  • Shared model of the underlying business system
  • Comparable platform and architecture tradeoffs
  • Department evidence tied to demonstrated workflows
  • Preferred platform, risk profile, and execution requirements

The project gave leadership a defensible view of which architecture best supported the company and what execution would require. The preferred direction remained available when the organization was ready to shift investment from near-term production expansion to its next phase of technology transformation.

The lasting lesson was not that ERP selection is organizational design disguised as software selection. It was more precise: technology choices define which business-system responsibilities the company must own.
